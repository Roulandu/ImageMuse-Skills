# Spatial Photography Engine

Resolve the image in this order:

`visual thesis → scene skeleton → subject placement and support → camera position → projection and perspective → framing and focal length → occlusion and depth of field → motivated light → color and material → visible styling detail`

Composition names summarize the result; they never replace spatial decisions.

## Scene skeleton

Define:

- the subject's exact zone in the location;
- support surfaces and their height/orientation;
- one key environmental anchor;
- foreground, subject plane, prop plane, and background order;
- approximate near/middle/far distances;
- the scene axis and where it leads;
- which detail must remain readable and which may fall out of focus.

Make the environment act on the subject. Wind affects hair and fabric; wet pavement returns fill; window frames divide light; steps change weight and garment fall; held objects show grip, pressure, and contact.

## Subject placement and force

Specify only what the framing can show:

- subject position and approximate frame occupancy;
- headroom, foot room, gaze room, and movement room;
- body direction, face direction, and gaze direction separately;
- weight-bearing foot, free foot, support hand, lean point, or seated contact;
- shoulder, ribcage, and pelvis relationship when pose is important;
- contact shadow and believable center of gravity;
- motion direction and secondary response in hair, garment, dust, water, or fragments.

Do not describe shoes, socks, ankle jewelry, or lower garments in a tight portrait where they cannot be visible. Conversely, define footwear, hem behavior, and ground contact in a full-length image.

## Camera coordinates

Choose the camera before the focal length:

1. framing and required environmental coverage;
2. camera height relative to eyes, chest, waist, or knees;
3. camera-to-subject distance;
4. yaw, pitch, and intentional roll;
5. shooting axis relative to the scene axis;
6. horizon height and principal vanishing point;
7. focal length needed to obtain the framing from that position.

Perspective is controlled primarily by camera position and distance. Focal length controls angle of view; apparent compression follows when the camera moves back to maintain framing. Never claim a lens has “zero distortion.”

## Projection and perspective

State:

- one-, two-, or three-point perspective when architecture matters;
- the principal vanishing point and which lines converge toward it;
- which near object or body part receives scale emphasis;
- whether verticals remain parallel or intentionally converge;
- whether perspective correction is desired;
- how subject, landmark, and background scale relate.

Avoid tangencies: do not let poles, hoops, frames, signs, or horizon lines appear to grow from the head or sever a joint.

## Occlusion and focus

For every foreground or overlapping element, define what it may cover. Keep eyes, face, support points, key garment structure, and narrative anchor readable unless concealment is the thesis.

Allocate sharpness by narrative role:

- first read: sharpest or highest local contrast;
- second read: readable but subordinate;
- afterimage: soft, partial, reflected, shadowed, or near the frame edge.

Choose aperture and depth of field only after subject distance, background distance, framing, and required environmental readability are known.

## Motivated lighting and exposure

Every principal light must have a believable source in or beyond the scene. Specify:

- source and position;
- apparent size, diffusion, and softness;
- key/fill relationship and any negative fill;
- subject-to-background exposure difference;
- ambient/flash balance when relevant;
- white-balance and mixed-color-temperature intent;
- highlight protection, black-floor intention, and catchlight when visible;
- material-specific response and consistent shadow direction.

Use fewer, stronger lighting decisions. “Three-point lighting,” “cinematic side light,” or “soft premium light” alone is insufficient.

## Visibility-driven styling

After space, camera, and light are fixed, describe visible details in this order:

1. expression and skin behavior under the chosen light;
2. hair silhouette, movement, and flyaways;
3. makeup emphasis that remains visible at the chosen framing;
4. garment silhouette, color, construction, fit, and primary material;
5. lower garment, hem, legs, footwear, socks/hosiery, and ankle detail only when visible;
6. one or two accessories with compositional or narrative purpose.

Avoid encyclopedic styling. Each visible detail must support identity, hierarchy, material dialogue, movement, or task purpose.
